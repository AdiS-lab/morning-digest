U.S. announces new AI chip export controls. Overseas firms need permits to sell compute to China.

The loophole the rule targets

The U.S. Trade Representative announced on Monday that overseas companies supplying cloud computing and AI processing services will now require a U.S. government permit before renting or selling computing capacity to Chinese customers. The rule takes effect in 60 days and targets foreign data centers and cloud providers that use American-made chips and software and then resell access to Chinese firms seeking to train large AI models. Officials said the measure was designed to close a gap in the 2022 and 2023 chip export controls that China had been exploiting by routing compute purchases through third countries, particularly in Singapore, the UAE, and Malaysia. Under previous rules, the chip could not be sold to China directly, but the cloud service built on that chip could be sold to anyone.

The rule relies on a legal theory that the underlying computing hardware, being subject to U.S. export controls, makes any service derived from it similarly subject to U.S. jurisdiction regardless of where the service is delivered. Commerce Department officials said the threshold for requiring a permit was set at any workload exceeding 10,000 GPU-hours per month delivered to a Chinese entity, a level calibrated to catch large-scale AI training runs while exempting ordinary commercial cloud services. Permits would be granted or denied within 30 days, and the department said it expected to approve most requests from companies with no China-based ownership or financial ties.

Global reaction

Major cloud providers in Singapore, the UAE, and Malaysia said the rule would impose significant compliance costs and create legal uncertainty for existing contracts with Chinese customers. Singapore's trade ministry said it was seeking legal analysis of whether the rule was consistent with WTO obligations and that it would consult with Washington before deciding on a response. The UAE said it expected its cloud operators to comply with applicable laws but noted that it had not been consulted during the rule's drafting. China's Ministry of Commerce condemned the move as an illegal extraterritorial application of U.S. law and said Beijing would take countermeasures without specifying what they would be.

American semiconductor companies saw shares fall modestly on the news, with investors uncertain about downstream demand effects on cloud providers in affected countries. Nvidia's stock fell 2.1 percent and Broadcom fell 1.8 percent. The EU expressed concern about the extraterritorial scope of the rule and said it would consult with Washington before deciding whether European cloud providers operating in third countries would be required to comply. French officials said the rule as written could affect European companies operating data centers in the Gulf, and that Brussels would push for carve-outs for companies with no connection to U.S. persons or government contracts.

What it means for AI development

The rule puts overseas cloud providers in a difficult commercial position. Companies in Singapore and the UAE have invested billions of dollars in GPU clusters partly on the assumption that Chinese demand, routed through their platforms, would justify the capital expenditure. Under the new rule, any customer that exceeds the 10,000 GPU-hour threshold and is based in China will require a U.S. permit, adding friction and legal risk to those contracts. Analysts said most large-scale AI training workloads already exceed that threshold and that the effective result for Chinese AI labs would be a sharp reduction in accessible overseas compute within 60 days.

China's domestic chip industry has been racing to fill the gap left by earlier export controls, with Huawei's Ascend line and several smaller domestic GPU makers shipping products that trail leading American chips by roughly two years in performance. The new rule may accelerate investment in Chinese domestic alternatives while also pushing Chinese AI labs to become more efficient with smaller compute budgets, an approach that some researchers argue has already forced innovations in model architecture that Western labs have had to study and adopt. The Commerce Department said it expected Chinese domestic compute capacity to remain insufficient to fully replace U.S.-chip-based overseas access for at least 18 to 24 months.
