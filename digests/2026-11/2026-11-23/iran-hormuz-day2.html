Iran's Hormuz ultimatum enters Day 2. U.S. rejects terms and issues counter-proposal.

The strait goes dark

Iran's 72-hour ultimatum to close the Strait of Hormuz unless all U.S. military operations in the Gulf cease entered its second day on Monday with the closure already in effect. Commercial shipping tracking services recorded only two vessel transits on Sunday, against a long-run daily average of roughly 85. The tankers that did attempt passage reported receiving radio warnings in Farsi from vessels the U.S. Navy identified as IRGC fast boats operating out of Bandar Abbas. Lloyd's of London has suspended its marine war-risk insurance for the strait, and multiple major shipping lines have rerouted vessels around the Cape of Good Hope, adding roughly 14 days to delivery times for Gulf petroleum cargoes.

Iran's foreign minister said in a televised address on Monday morning that Washington had shown no seriousness and that the clock was running. The Supreme National Security Council issued a statement saying the Islamic Republic would act on its stated deadline at 2200 Tehran time on Tuesday unless conditions changed. Oil held above $145 per barrel, its highest level since 2008. Gulf states, which export roughly 20 percent of globally traded oil through the strait, are under intense pressure from customers in Asia and Europe to find a path to reopening before the deadline expires.

Washington's counter-offer

The U.S. rejected Iran's demand for a full halt to Gulf operations, calling it incompatible with its treaty obligations to allies in the region. The State Department announced a counter-proposal giving Tehran four days to agree to a new framework covering three elements: a temporary arrangement for the strait, a resumption of nuclear talks under a modified JCPOA architecture, and a phased rollback of some secondary sanctions. Qatar's foreign minister flew to Tehran on Sunday night and Oman's deputy foreign minister was in Washington on Monday morning carrying the positions of both sides, but diplomats in both capitals described the gap as wide. Iran's condition for entering any framework talks is a permanent end to U.S. military operations in the Gulf, which Washington has refused to offer.

The Pentagon confirmed the USS George Washington carrier group was now on station in the Arabian Sea alongside a second carrier group already in the region. Minesweeping assets were being prepositioned off the Omani coast. The Joint Chiefs told Congress in a classified briefing that any attempt to force-reopen the strait would require at least 10 to 14 days of military operations and would likely trigger wider Iranian retaliation against U.S. bases in Bahrain, Kuwait, and Qatar. Senior senators on both sides said after the briefing that they expected the administration to avoid military action unless Iran struck first.
