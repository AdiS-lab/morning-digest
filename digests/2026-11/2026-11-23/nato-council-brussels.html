NATO holds emergency session in Brussels over Russian missiles in Poland.

An alliance on edge

The North Atlantic Council convened an extraordinary session at NATO headquarters in Brussels on Monday, its first formal Article 4 consultation since Friday night when two Russian cruise missiles and a drone came down on Polish territory near the Ukrainian border. Secretary-General Mark Rutte told reporters after the session that the violations were dangerous and unacceptable and that NATO would defend every inch of Polish territory. Poland's deputy defense minister Pawel Zalevski presented radar tracking data showing that the objects had altered course after crossing from Ukrainian airspace, strengthening Poland's argument that the strikes were deliberate targeting rather than stray munitions from Russia's mass attack on Ukraine.

The meeting drew all 32 NATO ambassadors and ran for nearly five hours. The Baltic states and Poland pushed for the council to issue a formal declaration that any further incursion into allied territory would trigger Article 5 collective defense obligations. Rutte declined, saying the alliance would not make that declaration until a joint NATO-Poland technical investigation was complete, which he expected to take ten days. Russia's foreign ministry issued a statement Monday calling the incident a fabrication orchestrated by Ukrainian intelligence to drag the West into a direct military confrontation.

What NATO decided

Poland asked the alliance for two additional Patriot air defense batteries to be deployed to its eastern flank within 30 days. Germany and the Netherlands each pledged one battery from their own inventories on a rotational basis. The batteries are expected to arrive within three weeks and will be positioned to cover the border region where the missiles came down. France said it was reviewing whether to contribute air assets for additional coverage over the same area. The U.S. said the existing two Patriot batteries it already had deployed in Poland would be supplemented by additional radar and command systems transferred from stocks in Germany.

Rutte said Article 5 consultations, which would formally invoke the collective defense clause, could not begin until the joint NATO-Poland technical investigation was complete. The Baltic states called for a NATO ministerial meeting this week, and Estonia's foreign minister said publicly that the alliance was applying a double standard by treating Russian missiles landing on allied territory differently from Russian missiles landing on Ukrainian cities. No extraordinary ministerial meeting was confirmed as of Monday evening.

The road to Article 5

The legal and political question hanging over the session is whether the two missiles and the drone constituted an attack on NATO territory that would trigger Article 5, or whether they are best handled as a serious but potentially unintended escalation. Alliance officials said privately that most members were reluctant to invoke Article 5 without clearer evidence of deliberate targeting of a NATO member state rather than weapons that overshot Ukraine. The ten-day investigation period is designed to give diplomats time to assess the evidence and give Russia an opportunity to issue a formal explanation, which Moscow has so far refused to do.

Ukraine's president said in a nightly address that Kyiv possessed radar and satellite data proving the missiles were deliberately redirected toward Poland and invited NATO investigators to review the material. The U.S. said it was analyzing its own sensor data and would share findings with the investigation team. The critical question for the alliance is whether the incident represents a one-off navigational or guidance failure, a deliberate probe of NATO's resolve, or a signal that Russia is prepared to widen the war's geographic scope.
