COP31 ends with a weak compromise. Gulf states and Russia reject the final text.

Three days of overtime, then a split

Climate negotiations in Antalya, Turkey, concluded Sunday morning after 74 hours of overtime, producing a final agreement adopted by 174 of 198 parties. The text removes the hard fossil fuel phase-out language that developing nations and the EU pushed for, replacing it with a voluntary transition pathway with no binding enforcement mechanism. The core finance deal establishes a $90 billion annual climate fund for the Global South, well below the $120 billion floor demanded by the African Group and the Alliance of Small Island States. Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Russia, and Iran formally objected to the finance provisions and declined to adopt the text. India and Brazil co-signed a separate declaration calling the outcome insufficient but agreed not to block it.

The COP31 host, Turkey, had wagered significant diplomatic capital on producing any agreement at all. Turkish foreign minister Hakan Fidan claimed the result as a success for Ankara, pointing to the creation of a new Loss and Damage disbursement committee that Turkey will chair through 2028. The EU's climate envoy Wopke Hoekstra said the agreement, while far from adequate, preserved the multilateral framework at a moment when geopolitical fractures threatened to end it entirely. The UN Secretary-General called it a floor, not a ceiling, and urged nations to revise their national targets upward before COP32 in Nairobi.

What the text says and what it does not say

The agreement contains three operative sections. The first endorses a voluntary fossil fuel transition pathway that calls on nations to reduce oil and gas production in a manner consistent with national circumstances. There is no sector-specific timeline. The second creates the $90 billion climate fund, with contributions assessed on a formula that weights historical emissions and current GDP. The third establishes a Loss and Damage disbursement committee with a mandate to pay out from an existing $700 million fund. No new money was committed for Loss and Damage beyond what was already pledged at COP28. The word phase-out does not appear anywhere in the final text.

Scientists affiliated with the IPCC said the voluntary pathway is incompatible with limiting warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius. A coalition of 47 small island states issued a joint statement calling the outcome a death sentence written in diplomatic language. Environmental groups outside the convention hall burned copies of the final text. Inside, delegates from several African nations wept during the closing plenary.

What comes next

The presidency of COP32 passes to Kenya, which will host the summit in Nairobi in November 2027. Kenya's environment minister said she would use the intervening year to build a broader coalition for binding commitments, particularly among African and Asian nations. The EU announced it would begin bilateral talks with India, Brazil, and South Africa to coordinate a push for stronger language in Nairobi. The United States, whose delegation attended in a reduced capacity, issued a one-paragraph statement welcoming the preservation of the multilateral process without committing to any specific follow-up action.

The four nations that rejected the text, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Russia, and Iran, issued a joint statement saying they would not contribute to the new $90 billion fund and called for a parallel process outside the UNFCCC framework. Energy analysts said the rejection by major oil producers significantly reduces the fund's practical viability. The first disbursements are not expected before 2028 at the earliest, and critics noted that the assessment formula has yet to be finalized. The 1.5 degree target remains in the preamble of the agreement, but no mechanism exists to enforce it.
