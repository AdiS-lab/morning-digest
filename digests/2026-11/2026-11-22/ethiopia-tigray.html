Ethiopia and Eritrea exchange artillery fire along their border. First clashes since 2000.

Shots fired at Zalambessa

Ethiopian and Eritrean forces exchanged artillery fire at two points along their shared border in the Tigray region on Saturday night, in the first armed clash between the two countries since the 1998-2000 border war. The heaviest exchange occurred at the Zalambessa crossing, the same location where fighting began in 1998. Ethiopia's government blamed Eritrea for opening fire first and said its forces had responded to defend Ethiopian territory. Eritrea's information minister said Ethiopian forces had entered a buffer zone established under the 2000 Algiers Agreement and been repelled by Eritrean border troops. Neither government disclosed troop positions or the number of artillery rounds fired. No casualties were acknowledged on either side, though the UN said it could not confirm conditions on the ground.

The UN's Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs said it suspended aid convoys into northern Tigray as a precaution following the exchange. Around 180,000 people in that area remain dependent on humanitarian food assistance from the World Food Program. The African Union's Peace and Security Council announced it would hold an emergency session Monday to address the incident. The United States and European Union issued a joint statement calling for immediate restraint and direct dialogue between Addis Ababa and Asmara. Neither side had responded to that call by Sunday evening.

Why relations collapsed after 2022

The 2018 peace deal between Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed and Eritrean President Isaias Afwerki, which won Abiy the Nobel Peace Prize, unraveled over Eritrea's role in the 2020-2022 Tigray civil war. Eritrea sent troops into Tigray to fight alongside the Ethiopian federal army against the Tigray People's Liberation Front, the TPLF, and Eritrean forces were accused of serious atrocities including mass executions and the deliberate destruction of towns. The Pretoria Agreement that ended the Tigray civil war in November 2022 required Eritrean forces to withdraw from Ethiopia, but Eritrea refused to sign the agreement and Afwerki never acknowledged that Eritrean troops were present at all.

Since 2023, relations between Addis Ababa and Asmara have deteriorated sharply. Abiy accused Eritrea of continuing to supply weapons to remnant TPLF factions in northern Tigray. Eritrea accused Ethiopia of allowing the TPLF to regroup with the aim of eventually destabilizing the Eritrean government. Both sides began moving additional troops to the border region in October 2026, a buildup first reported publicly on November 18. The Saturday night artillery exchange at Zalambessa, the precise location where the 1998 war started, is seen by regional analysts as deeply symbolic and potentially deliberate. Both countries have fully mobilized armies and have fought two major wars in the past 30 years.

Humanitarian and regional stakes

A renewed Ethiopia-Eritrea conflict would be the second major war on the African continent in active escalation this year, alongside the ongoing conflict in Sudan. Northern Tigray remains one of the most food-insecure regions in the world, with harvests still recovering from destruction during the 2020-2022 civil war. Any new fighting along the border would likely disrupt the last functioning supply route into northern Tigray from Eritrea's Red Sea coast, which some aid groups have used to bypass contested areas inside Ethiopia. The WFP said it had enough supplies prepositioned for about three weeks and would monitor the situation before deciding whether to resume convoys.

Regional leaders in neighboring Kenya, Uganda, and Djibouti said they were following the situation with concern. Djibouti hosts U.S., French, Chinese, and Japanese military bases and would be directly affected by any broader conflict that destabilized the Horn of Africa. The United States Africa Command said it was monitoring the situation but had no comment on force posture. The AU Peace and Security Council's Monday session will be its first emergency convening on this topic since the Tigray war. Analysts said the council has limited enforcement tools and that the key variable is whether Abiy and Afwerki choose to escalate or stand down in the next 48 hours.
