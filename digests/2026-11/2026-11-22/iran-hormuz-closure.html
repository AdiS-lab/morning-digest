Iran announces 72-hour ultimatum to close the Strait of Hormuz. Oil spikes above $140.

The ultimatum

Iran's Supreme National Security Council issued a formal statement Sunday morning giving the United States 72 hours to halt all air and naval operations in the Persian Gulf or face a full closure of the Strait of Hormuz to commercial and military traffic. The statement cited 34 IRGC personnel killed in U.S. strikes over the past two weeks and described the closure as a lawful act of self-defense under the UN Charter. Iran has the ability to close the strait using a combination of naval mines, anti-ship missiles, and fast-attack craft operating from islands at the strait's narrowest point, a 33-kilometer channel through which roughly 21 percent of the world's oil supply passes daily.

The announcement sent Brent crude to $141 per barrel within hours, its highest price since the conflict began. Lloyd's of London suspended new marine insurance policies for Hormuz transits effective immediately, citing force majeure. Shipping data from Lloyd's List Intelligence showed 14 tankers turning around in the Arabian Sea within six hours of the announcement. Qatar, which exports liquefied natural gas through the strait, issued an urgent appeal for de-escalation and said it was in contact with both Washington and Tehran.

The U.S. response and military posture

The U.S. Fifth Fleet based in Bahrain said it would not alter operations and that any attempt to close the Strait of Hormuz would be treated as an act of war requiring an immediate military response. The Pentagon announced it was moving an additional carrier strike group, the USS George Washington and its escort vessels, into the Arabian Sea to join the USS Abraham Lincoln already stationed there. That brings the number of U.S. carrier strike groups in the region to two, the highest concentration since the 2003 Iraq War. Defense Secretary Hegseth said the United States had sufficient munitions and air assets to keep the strait open by force if necessary.

Qatar and Oman, both of which depend on Hormuz transit for their own energy exports, issued urgent statements urging both sides to stand down. UAE officials held emergency consultations with the U.S. ambassador. The European Union convened an emergency energy security meeting in Brussels and activated its gas storage coordination mechanism, which allows member states to share strategic reserves. Germany and France said they were in contact with Iranian officials through back channels and appealed for a 48-hour pause to allow diplomacy to proceed. Iran did not publicly respond to those appeals by Sunday evening.

Stakes and precedents

Iran has threatened to close the strait before, most prominently in 2012 and 2019, but has never followed through with a full closure. This ultimatum differs in that it comes amid active combat operations between Iranian and American forces, with IRGC personnel already dead from U.S. strikes. Analysts at the Center for Strategic and International Studies said the domestic pressure on Iran's leadership to respond after 34 deaths is significantly higher than in previous standoffs. The 72-hour window expires Tuesday evening local time.

A full closure would immediately affect roughly one-fifth of global oil supply and could trigger a recession in energy-importing economies within weeks. Insurance markets, which have already sharply raised premiums for Gulf transits over the past three weeks, would effectively cease to function for Hormuz traffic. The IEA said member states hold strategic reserves sufficient for about 90 days of consumption at current rates. Saudi Arabia said it was monitoring the situation and had not taken any supply decisions. Iraq, which exports most of its oil through Hormuz, did not comment publicly.
