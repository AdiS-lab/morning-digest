Two more tankers hit mines in the Strait of Hormuz

Saturday's incidents

Two oil tankers struck naval mines south of the Strait of Hormuz on Saturday and caught fire. Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps Navy said the vessels had been traveling along what it called an unauthorized route and had their automatic identification systems switched off, a practice used to avoid tracking. The IRGC said the ships entered Iran's declared exclusion zone and that it bore no responsibility for what followed. No casualties were immediately confirmed.

One vessel, the Panamanian-flagged MT Sirius Star II, lost engine power and began listing. The crew of 22 was evacuated by a nearby merchant vessel before the ship was taken under tow by Iranian naval vessels. The second tanker, registered in the Marshall Islands, sustained damage to its bow and reached the port of Fujairah in the United Arab Emirates under its own power with a small fire still burning on deck.

Pattern of incidents since November 5

Saturday's strikes follow three tankers set on fire on November 12 in the same general area, and two additional incidents on November 5 that opened the current crisis. In total, seven tankers have been damaged in Hormuz-area mine strikes since the start of November. The U.S. Navy's Fifth Fleet, based in Bahrain, has warned commercial shipping to avoid the southern approaches to the strait and use the escorted convoy system it has operated since November 7.

Iran has not formally claimed responsibility for placing mines but has said vessels violating its exclusion zone do so at their own risk. The U.S., the United Kingdom, and France have called the incidents deliberate acts of maritime sabotage and said they hold Iran responsible. Insurance premiums for tankers transiting the Persian Gulf have risen sharply since early November, and several large shipping companies have suspended sailings through the strait.

Diplomatic and economic consequences

The cumulative effect of seven tanker incidents in ten days has pushed Brent crude above $112 per barrel, the highest since 2022. Saudi Arabia, which routes most of its exports through the Strait of Hormuz, has quietly asked the U.S. for additional naval escorts. Qatar, which reached out to Iran last week through back channels, said its mediation effort had stalled after Saturday's strikes.

The incidents are expected to dominate the agenda at the planned Trump-Xi-Putin summit in Shenzhen on November 19. Iran's oil exports, which pass through the same strait the IRGC is mining, have so far not been targeted, though analysts said the situation raised the risk of miscalculation. The U.S. has not publicly stated a threshold for a direct military response.
