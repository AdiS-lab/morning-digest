Iran's Hormuz ultimatum enters Day 2. U.S. rejects terms and issues counter-proposal.

Iran's 72-hour ultimatum to close the Strait of Hormuz unless U.S. military operations in the Gulf cease entered its second day on Monday, with the strait already effectively shut to commercial shipping. Tracking data shows only two vessels transited on Sunday, against a normal daily average of roughly 85. Iran's foreign minister said the clock was running and that Washington had shown "no seriousness," while the Supreme National Security Council said Iran would act on its stated deadline unless conditions changed. Oil held above $145 per barrel and Lloyd's of London kept its marine insurance suspension in place.

The U.S. rejected Iran's demand for a full halt to Gulf operations and presented a counter-proposal giving Tehran four days to agree to a new framework covering the strait, Iran's nuclear program, and sanctions. Qatar and Oman are carrying the two sides' positions back and forth, but diplomats in both capitals described the gap as wide. The Pentagon said the USS George Washington carrier group was now on station in the Arabian Sea alongside a second carrier group already in the region, and that it was prepositioning minesweeping assets off the Omani coast.

NATO holds emergency session in Brussels over Russian missiles in Poland.

The North Atlantic Council convened an extraordinary session at NATO headquarters in Brussels on Monday, its first formal Article 4 meeting since the Friday incident in which two Russian cruise missiles and a drone came down on Polish territory. Secretary-General Mark Rutte told reporters after the meeting that the violations were dangerous and unacceptable and that NATO would defend every inch of Polish territory. Poland's deputy defense minister Pawel Zalevski presented radar data showing the objects had altered course after crossing from Ukraine, strengthening Poland's argument that the strikes were deliberate rather than stray munitions.

Poland asked the alliance for two additional Patriot air defense batteries to be deployed to its eastern flank within 30 days. Germany and the Netherlands said they would each contribute one battery from their own inventories on a rotational basis. The Baltic states called for a NATO ministerial meeting this week and pushed for a formal declaration that any further incursion into allied territory would trigger Article 5. Rutte said Article 5 consultations could not begin until a joint NATO-Poland technical investigation was complete, which he expected to take ten days. Russia's foreign ministry issued a statement Monday calling the incident a fabrication orchestrated by Ukrainian intelligence.

Ukraine strikes Russian ship in the Baltic and a warship in a Libyan port.

Ukraine struck a Russian-flagged cargo vessel in the Baltic Sea on Saturday and hit a Russian frigate moored at the port of Tobruk in eastern Libya on Sunday, in what Ukrainian military intelligence described as coordinated long-range drone operations. The cargo ship, which Ukraine said had been delivering military supplies to Kaliningrad, sustained damage to its stern and was towed to a Swedish anchorage, where Swedish coastguard said it was not leaking fuel. The frigate in Tobruk was described by satellite imagery analysts as the Grigorovich-class vessel Admiral Essen, which had been using Libyan ports as a resupply stop.

The strikes represent a significant geographic expansion of Ukraine's campaign beyond its own borders and the Black Sea. Russia's defense ministry acknowledged an attack on a vessel in the Baltic but said the drone had been intercepted and damage was minor. It did not comment on the Libya strike. Sweden said it was investigating the Baltic incident and was in contact with Ukraine. Libya's internationally recognized government in Tripoli said it had not authorized Russian naval vessels to use Tobruk and demanded an explanation from both Russia and Ukraine. NATO allies privately expressed concern about the Libya strike's implications for Mediterranean security.

African Union convenes over Ethiopia-Eritrea. Neither side agrees to direct talks.

The African Union's Peace and Security Council met in emergency session in Addis Ababa on Monday to address Saturday night's artillery exchanges between Ethiopian and Eritrean forces along their shared border in the Tigray region. AU Commission Chair Moussa Faki Mahamat called for an immediate ceasefire and offered AU mediation. Ethiopia's government said it was open to AU engagement but would not negotiate directly with Asmara until Eritrean forces withdrew from what Addis Ababa described as illegally occupied buffer zone territory. Eritrea's information ministry issued a brief statement saying Ethiopia had provoked the incident and that Asmara would defend its sovereignty.

Fighting resumed at the Zalambessa crossing point on Monday morning, with both sides reporting exchanges of mortar fire. UN humanitarian officials said aid convoys to northern Tigray remained suspended for a third day, leaving approximately 180,000 people cut off from food and medicine deliveries. Egypt, which has its own tensions with Ethiopia over the Nile dam, issued a statement expressing support for Eritrea's position, drawing sharp criticism from Addis Ababa. The U.S. and EU issued separate statements urging restraint and backing AU mediation. No date was set for further talks.

U.S. announces new AI chip export controls. Overseas firms need permits to sell compute to China.

The U.S. Trade Representative announced on Monday that overseas companies supplying cloud computing and AI processing services will now require a U.S. government permit before renting or selling computing capacity to Chinese customers. The rule, effective in 60 days, targets foreign data centers and cloud providers that use American-made chips and software and then resell access to Chinese firms seeking to train large AI models. Officials said the measure was designed to close a loophole in earlier chip export controls that China had been exploiting by routing compute purchases through third countries, particularly in Southeast Asia and the Gulf.

Major cloud providers in Singapore, the UAE, and Malaysia said the rule would impose significant compliance costs and create legal uncertainty for existing contracts. China's Ministry of Commerce condemned the move as an illegal extraterritorial application of U.S. law and said Beijing would take countermeasures. American chip companies saw shares fall modestly on the news, with investors uncertain about downstream demand effects. The EU expressed concern about the extraterritorial scope of the rule and said it would consult with Washington before deciding whether to comply.
