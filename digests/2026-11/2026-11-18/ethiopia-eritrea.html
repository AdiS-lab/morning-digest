Ethiopia and Eritrea mass troops along border as war fears grow

The current standoff

Ethiopia and Eritrea have moved forces to within striking distance of each other along their shared border, according to the conflict monitors ACLED and International Crisis Group. The buildup is concentrated near Badme, a town in disputed territory that was at the center of the 1998-2000 war between the two countries. Artillery exchanges and armed clashes have been reported in the area since last week. Ethiopian drones targeted positions near Badme on November 12, and Eritrean forces responded with return artillery fire in what analysts said was the most serious direct exchange since the Pretoria ceasefire agreement in late 2022.

The escalation stems in part from Ethiopian military operations against Tigrayan rebel forces that have pushed fighting toward the Eritrean border. Eritrea had backed the Ethiopian federal government against the Tigray People's Liberation Front during the 2020-2022 Tigray war, but relations between Addis Ababa and Asmara have deteriorated since the Pretoria deal, which Eritrea was not party to and which left armed Tigrayan factions still active near the border.

Historical context

The Ethiopia-Eritrea border has been a fault line for decades. The two countries fought a devastating war from 1998 to 2000 that resulted in an estimated 70,000 to 100,000 military deaths on both sides. A formal peace agreement was signed in Algiers in 2000, but the border demarcation was never implemented and Badme's status remained unresolved. The relationship briefly normalized in 2018 when Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed signed a peace declaration with Eritrean President Isaias Afwerki, earning Abiy a Nobel Peace Prize. That rapprochement collapsed during the Tigray war, when both governments cooperated militarily but accumulated grievances over postwar arrangements.

The Pretoria agreement that ended the Tigray war in November 2022 required Eritrean forces to withdraw from Tigray, which Asmara resisted. Relations between the two governments have remained formally cool since then. Analysts said the current border massing reflects Eritrea's concern that continued Ethiopian operations in Tigray will produce a destabilized border zone that Asmara cannot control, combined with deeper suspicion that Addis Ababa no longer views Eritrea as a reliable partner.

Calls for de-escalation

Diplomats from the African Union and the United Nations have called on both governments to open communications channels and pull forces back from the border. The AU's Peace and Security Council is scheduled to hold an emergency session this week. The United States and European Union have issued statements urging restraint and offering to facilitate dialogue. Neither Addis Ababa nor Asmara has publicly responded to de-escalation calls, and no direct government-to-government communications have been confirmed as of Wednesday.
