Denmark approves Arctic security pact with U.S. and Greenland

The vote and what the pact covers

Denmark's parliament, the Folketing, voted 109 to 0 on Thursday to approve a trilateral security agreement with the United States and Greenland covering Arctic and North Atlantic defense. The agreement grants increased U.S. access to Greenlandic air and naval facilities, establishes joint patrol operations in Arctic and sub-Arctic waters, and commits the United States to infrastructure investment in Greenland including road, port, and communications upgrades. The vote came eight months after President Trump threatened to seize Greenland by force, a statement that shocked Copenhagen and prompted emergency diplomatic negotiations.

Greenland's Prime Minister Mute Egede called the agreement "a partnership, not a surrender." Denmark's foreign minister said the pact does not alter Greenland's constitutional status as an autonomous territory within the Kingdom of Denmark. The 70 members of parliament who did not vote were absent rather than opposed. No Danish lawmaker voted against the agreement.

Existing U.S. infrastructure and what changes

The United States already operates Pituffik Space Base, formerly Thule Air Base, in northwest Greenland under a 1951 defense agreement. Pituffik is the northernmost U.S. military installation and hosts ballistic missile early warning radar and satellite tracking systems. The new pact expands on the 1951 agreement by adding two additional airfield access rights at Kangerlussuaq and Narsarsuaq, joint use of a new deep-water port at Nuuk planned for completion in 2029, and a shared Arctic maritime patrol zone extending 200 nautical miles from Greenland's coast.

Background: Trump's threat and the negotiations

Trump first raised the idea of purchasing Greenland in 2019 during his first term. In March 2026 he said the U.S. would "take Greenland one way or another" to secure Arctic access, drawing formal protests from Denmark, the European Union, and NATO allies. Denmark immediately called an emergency parliamentary session and authorized direct talks with both Greenland's government and the U.S. State Department. The negotiations ran for five months, with Greenland's government insisting that any agreement preserve full autonomy and include direct economic benefits for Greenland rather than Denmark alone.

The final text includes a provision giving Greenland veto authority over any additional U.S. facility requests beyond those named in the pact. Denmark's prime minister called that clause "the guarantee that makes this agreement possible." Japan and Australia, which observe Arctic Council proceedings, issued statements welcoming the agreement as a contribution to Arctic stability.
