Democrat wins Mississippi Senate race, majority grows to 55

November 12, 2026

What happened

Democrat Scott Colom won the Mississippi U.S. Senate race on Wednesday, defeating the Republican incumbent in a narrow margin that was not called until nearly a week after Election Day. The win gives Democrats at least 55 seats in the incoming Senate. One race in Alaska remains uncalled. Mississippi has not elected a Democrat to the U.S. Senate since 1982.

Colom, a circuit court judge from Columbus, Mississippi, ran on a platform focused on healthcare access and rural economic development. He kept national Democratic figures at arm's length during the campaign, emphasizing local issues and his record as a judge. The Republican incumbent, Roger Wicker, had held the seat since 2007.

Why Mississippi matters

Mississippi is one of the most reliably Republican states in the country at the federal level. The last Democrat to hold a Mississippi Senate seat, John Stennis, retired in 1989. Colom's win reflects both a broader Democratic wave in the midterms and a campaign strategy that explicitly avoided national party branding. The Cook Political Report had rated the race as "Likely Republican" until late October, when internal polling showed it tightening.

The 55-seat majority gives Democrats a larger cushion than any Senate majority since 2009. With 55 seats, the caucus can absorb up to five defections on a party-line vote and still pass legislation. That is a meaningful shift from the razor-thin 51 and 52-seat majorities Democrats held in recent years, where a single objection by a moderate member could block legislation entirely.

What comes next

Even with 55 seats, Democrats still fall short of the 60 votes needed to break a filibuster. That means any significant legislation will require either Republican cooperation or a willingness to pursue budget reconciliation for provisions that qualify. Democratic leaders have not publicly discussed changing the filibuster threshold, though pressure from the left flank of the caucus to do so is likely to increase with a larger majority.

The Alaska race, still uncalled, involves a ranked-choice vote count that is not expected to conclude until late November. If Democrats win Alaska, the majority would reach 56 seats. Senate Democratic leadership is expected to begin committee assignment negotiations and leadership elections before the end of November regardless of the Alaska outcome.
