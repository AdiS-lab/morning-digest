Hegseth impeachment articles introduced in Congress

November 10, 2026

What happened

Republican Congressmember Thomas Massie of Kentucky introduced eight articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth on Monday. The articles accuse Hegseth of violating the War Powers Resolution of 1973 by continuing to order attacks on Iran after both chambers of Congress passed resolutions instructing the administration to cease military operations. The charges also cite "high crimes and misdemeanors" stemming from the conduct of unauthorized military strikes.

Massie, a libertarian-leaning Republican known for breaking with his party on military spending, is the most prominent member of his own party to bring impeachment articles against a Trump cabinet official. The filing marks the third impeachment effort against Trump officials in recent weeks, following two earlier attempts directed at Hegseth during his tenure and separate articles against other cabinet members. His action drew sharp condemnation from Republican leadership and mixed reactions from Democrats, some of whom expressed support for the underlying legal argument while declining to co-sponsor articles filed by a Republican.

Background

The War Powers Resolution of 1973 requires the president to notify Congress within 48 hours of committing U.S. armed forces to hostilities and limits deployments to 60 days without a formal authorization from Congress. The law was passed over President Nixon's veto during the Vietnam War. Every administration since has disputed aspects of its constitutionality, but no secretary of defense has ever been impeached for violating it.

The Trump administration launched strikes on Iranian military infrastructure in late October 2026, citing an imminent threat to American vessels transiting the Strait of Hormuz. After both the House and Senate passed concurrent resolutions ordering a halt, the White House argued those resolutions were non-binding policy expressions rather than enforceable law. Operations continued. That gap between what Congress ordered and what the executive did is the constitutional core of Massie's eight articles.

What happens next

The articles face an almost certain procedural block in the House. Speaker-designate Jim Jordan and the House Freedom Caucus have not endorsed the effort and are unlikely to schedule a floor vote. Under House rules, Massie can force a privileged resolution to the floor, but leadership can table it with a simple majority. The math is unfavorable: most Republicans oppose removing Hegseth, and Democrats who might support impeachment on principle have largely declined to co-sponsor articles authored by a Republican member.

Even if the full House voted to impeach, conviction in the Senate requires a two-thirds supermajority, roughly 20 Republican votes in the current chamber. Legal scholars say the articles raise genuine constitutional questions about the limits of executive war-making power, but removal from office under the present political alignment is considered effectively impossible.
