Senate Republicans suspend $350 billion Iran war defense bill

The bill and its collapse

Senate Republican leaders announced Thursday they are suspending efforts to pass a $350 billion supplemental defense package tied to the Iran war. Supporters said they lacked the votes to advance the bill before Democrats take control of the Senate in January. The legislation had been stalled for three weeks as moderate Republicans expressed concern about the total cost and the absence of an offsets plan. At least six Republican senators had said they would not vote for the bill without cuts to other discretionary spending to compensate for the new outlay.

The bill required 60 votes to advance past a procedural hurdle. Republican leaders could only count on 51 votes at most. With the Senate majority reversing in January and Democrats unlikely to revive a package of this structure, defense hawks described the collapse as a defining setback for the administration's war funding strategy. The Iran war has cost the U.S. military more than $48 billion since operations began in late February, according to Pentagon accounting figures submitted to Congress last month.

What the money would have funded

The package had four main components. The largest, at $120 billion, was earmarked for accelerated munitions production: new contracts with domestic manufacturers to expand output of precision-guided bombs, cruise missiles, and air defense interceptors. The second component, $85 billion, was for expanded naval operations in the Persian Gulf, covering fuel, maintenance, and personnel costs for the two carrier strike groups and supporting vessels deployed to the region. The third component, $65 billion, covered base hardening in Bahrain, Qatar, Kuwait, and Diego Garcia, including blast barriers, underground storage, and missile defense improvements. The fourth component added $55 billion for new air defense battery purchases.

Republican defense hawks said the decision leaves the military without additional production funding during an active conflict. Senate Democrats, who will hold a 55-seat majority next year, have not committed to reviving a package in this form. The incoming Democratic leadership signaled it would want any new Iran war supplemental tied to a formal war powers authorization debate, which the administration has so far resisted.
