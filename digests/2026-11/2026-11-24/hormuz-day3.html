Iran orders Hormuz closed "further day by day." Sets seven conditions. Qatar seeks a last-ditch deal.

November 24, 2026

The closure tightens

Iran's army commander-in-chief ordered the navy to close the Strait of Hormuz "further day by day" until Washington met Tehran's demands. The 72-hour ultimatum Iran issued on November 22 expired without any agreement, and Iran showed no sign of backing down. The Revolutionary Guard Corps Navy issued fresh warnings against unauthorized transit routes and placed 26 tugboats on a non-compliance blacklist. Tugboats guide supertankers through the strait's narrow shipping lanes, and without them most large tankers cannot safely navigate the passage. The Joint Maritime Information Centre reported only a handful of vessels transited Sunday, against a normal daily average of roughly 85.

Oil held above $145 per barrel on Monday, up from $141 when Iran first announced the closure on November 22. Shipping insurers suspended coverage for vessels attempting the strait without military escorts, and Lloyd's of London war risk premiums reached record levels. The White House National Security Council estimated the closure was costing the global economy approximately $7 billion per day in disrupted trade and elevated fuel costs. The five countries most dependent on Gulf oil exports, including Japan, South Korea, and India, issued a joint statement calling for immediate restoration of freedom of navigation.

Seven conditions and the diplomatic effort

Iran issued a formal list of seven conditions for any negotiations: a full halt to U.S. naval operations in the Persian Gulf; lifted sanctions on the Iranian economy; release of approximately $6 billion in frozen Iranian funds held in South Korean and Japanese banks; U.S. recognition of Iran's right to civilian nuclear power; a formal declaration ending hostilities; withdrawal of U.S. forces from Bahrain; and an end to what Tehran called "hostile interference" in its internal affairs. The U.S. rejected all seven terms as non-starters. Secretary of State Rubio called the list "designed to prevent negotiation, not enable it."

Qatar's prime minister flew to Washington and met separately with Vice President Vance and Secretary Rubio, offering to carry a revised framework to Tehran. Qatar has served as the primary back-channel between Washington and Tehran through the Hormuz crisis. The U.S. counter-proposal reportedly involved a 90-day suspension of the closure in exchange for preliminary nuclear talks. Iran had not publicly responded by Monday evening. The Pentagon said two carrier groups were on station in the Arabian Sea and that minesweeping assets were being prepositioned off the Omani coast.
