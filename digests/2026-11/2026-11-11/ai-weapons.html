Religious leaders demand international regulation of "killer robots"

November 11, 2026

The letter

More than 32 leaders from Catholic, Protestant, Orthodox, and other Christian organizations signed an open letter addressed to UK Foreign Secretary David Lammy on Monday, calling for the British government to lead an international effort to ban or strictly regulate autonomous weapons systems that use artificial intelligence to identify and engage targets without meaningful human control. The letter was coordinated by the Religion Media Centre and represents one of the broadest coalitions of Christian voices on a security technology issue in recent years.

The signatories include the Archbishop of York, the head of the Catholic Bishops' Conference of England and Wales, senior figures from the Greek and Russian Orthodox churches in Britain, and leaders of the United Reformed Church, the Methodist Church, and several evangelical organizations. The letter describes autonomous weapons as "an affront to human dignity" and argues that delegating lethal decisions to algorithms violates fundamental ethical principles shared across Christian traditions about the sanctity of life and the requirement for human moral accountability in the use of force.

What they want

The letter makes three specific asks. First, the UK government should champion a binding international treaty at the United Nations that prohibits weapons systems capable of selecting and engaging human targets without a human operator making the final decision. Second, the government should suspend export licenses for components and software used in autonomous weapons development pending such a treaty. Third, British universities and defense contractors should be encouraged to adopt voluntary codes of conduct prohibiting research that removes human oversight from lethal systems.

The signatories acknowledge that defining "meaningful human control" is legally and technically contested. The letter does not demand a ban on AI-assisted targeting, which already exists in many forms, but specifically focuses on systems where the AI makes the final engagement decision with no human in the loop. This distinction mirrors the language used by the International Committee of the Red Cross in its own guidance on autonomous weapons, and places the religious coalition broadly within the mainstream of the humanitarian disarmament movement.

The broader debate

International talks on lethal autonomous weapons have been conducted under a UN Group of Governmental Experts since 2014, but have produced no binding agreement. Military powers including the United States, Russia, China, Israel, and South Korea have all resisted treaty language that would constrain operational autonomy. The UK has previously said it opposes fully autonomous weapons in principle but has not committed to a specific regulatory framework.

The religious letter lands at a moment when deployment is becoming practical rather than theoretical. Several countries are known to have fielded loitering munitions with varying degrees of autonomous targeting capability in active conflicts. The pace of deployment has outrun the pace of regulation, a gap the signatories describe as a moral emergency that governments cannot defer. Whether the letter moves UK policy is uncertain, but it adds institutional Christian voice to a debate that has largely been conducted by human rights lawyers, military ethicists, and arms control specialists.
