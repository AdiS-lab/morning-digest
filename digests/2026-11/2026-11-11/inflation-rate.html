U.S. inflation exceeds expectations, Fed rate hike likely

November 11, 2026

The October data

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Monday that the U.S. Consumer Price Index rose 3.9 percent year-over-year in October 2026, exceeding the 3.6 percent that Wall Street economists had forecast. Month-over-month, prices rose 0.4 percent, also above expectations. The core CPI, which strips out food and energy, came in at 3.4 percent annually, suggesting the inflation pressure is not confined to volatile commodity categories.

The October reading is the highest CPI print since April and reverses three months of gradual cooling. Categories driving the increase included shelter costs, which rose 5.1 percent annually, motor vehicle insurance up 11 percent, and medical care services up 4.3 percent. Food prices held relatively steady while energy costs, boosted by ongoing disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz region, contributed meaningfully to the monthly figure.

Fed response

Federal Reserve officials were widely expected to hold interest rates steady through the end of the year after a series of cuts in the summer. The October inflation data has shifted market expectations sharply. Fed funds futures now price in a 72 percent probability of a 25 basis point rate hike at the December FOMC meeting, up from 18 percent before the report. Several Fed governors are scheduled to speak this week, and markets will be watching their language for any shift in forward guidance.

Fed Chair Jerome Powell does not speak publicly until Thursday. The last time the Fed hiked rates after a period of pausing was in 2023, and the institutional reluctance to reverse course without clear evidence of re-acceleration means the December decision will be close. Some economists argue the October data is a one-month outlier driven by shelter cost lags and energy disruptions rather than a genuine re-acceleration of underlying inflation, and that the Fed should wait for November data before moving.

Market reaction

Equities fell sharply after the report. The S&P 500 dropped 1.4 percent, the Nasdaq fell 1.9 percent, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average lost over 400 points by mid-morning. The sell-off came just one day after markets had staged a broad recovery, underscoring the fragility of recent gains. Treasury yields rose, with the 10-year hitting 4.65 percent, the highest level since the summer.

Higher rates would raise borrowing costs for consumers and corporations at a moment when the economy is already absorbing elevated housing costs and reduced consumer confidence. Retailers and homebuilders were among the hardest hit sectors in Monday's sell-off. Analysts at several investment banks revised their year-end S&P 500 targets downward in the hours following the inflation release.
