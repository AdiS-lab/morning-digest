FAA grounds all U.S. flights after telecommunications outage

The ground stop

The Federal Aviation Administration issued a nationwide ground stop at 3:54 p.m. Eastern on Friday after a data overload knocked out radar and communications at air route traffic control centers across the country. Some 4,042 flights were airborne when the order came through. All inbound and outbound departures at U.S. airports were halted simultaneously. The stop lasted five hours and eleven minutes, the longest continuous grounding of U.S. aviation since September 11, 2001.

The failure originated at terminal radar approach control facilities, the low-altitude radar centers that guide planes on final approach and initial departure. Controllers at multiple ARTCC facilities reported simultaneous loss of data feeds from neighboring centers, leaving coverage gaps across the northeastern and southeastern United States. The FAA described the problem as a data overload rather than a hardware outage. No aircraft were in danger, officials said, because controllers were able to hold planes on the ground before gaps in radar coverage became critical.

Response and aftermath

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy confirmed the cause at a 6:30 p.m. briefing, saying the outage began as a telecommunications failure at TRACON facilities and cascaded through the national airspace data network. He said the FAA had identified the specific data pathway that failed and was applying an interim fix. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said federal investigators found no evidence of a cyberattack. The FAA lifted the ground stop at 10:15 p.m. Eastern, reopening the airspace region by region as radar feeds were restored.

Airlines were still working through thousands of cancellations and rebooking requests into Saturday morning. Delta and United each reported more than 800 cancellations. Airports in New York, Chicago, and Atlanta faced the longest ripple delays. The FAA said a full independent investigation would take several weeks and that interim fixes had been deployed overnight to prevent a recurrence. Members of the Senate Commerce Committee called for a hearing, with several citing the Microsoft outage from the previous day as evidence of systemic fragility in critical U.S. infrastructure.
