Iran orders Hormuz closed "further day by day." Sets seven conditions. Qatar seeks a last-ditch deal.

Iran's army commander-in-chief ordered the navy to close the Strait of Hormuz "further day by day" until Washington met Tehran's demands, with the 72-hour ultimatum now expired and no agreement in place. Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps Navy warned ships against unauthorized routes and placed 26 tugboats on a non-compliance blacklist, tightening pressure on the ecosystem of vessels that keep tanker traffic moving through the strait. Oil held above $145 per barrel. The Joint Maritime Information Centre said only a handful of vessels transited Sunday, against a normal daily average of roughly 85.

Iran issued a formal list of seven conditions for any negotiations, including a full halt to U.S. naval operations in the Gulf, lifted sanctions, released frozen funds, and a formal declaration ending hostilities. The U.S. rejected the terms as non-starters. Qatar's prime minister flew to Washington and met with Vice President Vance and Secretary of State Rubio, offering to carry a revised framework to Tehran. Diplomats in both capitals described the gap as wide. The Pentagon said two carrier groups were now on station in the Arabian Sea and that minesweeping assets were being prepositioned off the Omani coast.

Russia threatens NATO member Estonia with "open war" over border closure and alleged drone support for Ukraine.

Russia's Foreign Ministry declared Estonia was "on the verge of triggering open war" after Tallinn announced it would close its 183-mile land border with Russia on December 1 for six months. Moscow accused Estonia of allowing Ukrainian drones to use its airspace for attacks on Russia, obstructing Russian shipping in the Baltic Sea, and banning the Russian language in public life. Estonia denied every accusation and called the statement an attempt to deflect attention from Russia's own actions against European security. The border closure announcement followed Saturday's Ukrainian drone strike on a Russian cargo ship in the Gulf of Finland, which Finnish Prime Minister Petteri Orpo said showed the war was "spilling over" into the region.

NATO Secretary-General Rutte said the alliance was monitoring developments in Estonia closely and that any attack on a NATO member would trigger a collective response. The Baltic states called for a NATO ministerial meeting this week. Estonia's prime minister said the border would close as planned and that Moscow's threats would not change that decision. Military analysts said Russia lacked the capacity to open a new front against a NATO member while suffering losses exceeding 700,000 soldiers in Ukraine, but noted the rhetoric represented a sharp escalation in Russia's posture toward the Baltic states.

Jensen Huang returns as Nvidia CEO after Trump personally intervened with the board.

Nvidia's board reinstated Jensen Huang as president and chief executive officer on Sunday after President Trump called into a board meeting and personally pushed for his return, in a highly unusual intervention by an American president in a private company's governance. The board cited assurances from senior government officials that Huang posed no national security risk. Nvidia's stock surged roughly 30 percent on Monday, its largest single-day rally in decades. Huang had resigned eleven days earlier following published reports of extramarital affairs.

Trump confirmed in a CNBC interview that he had spoken with the board "directly and indirectly" and said the country needed Huang leading Nvidia. The move drew sharp criticism from corporate governance experts and from some in Trump's own party, which had just suffered heavy losses in the midterm elections. Critics said the president had no business directing who runs a publicly traded company. Supporters argued that Nvidia's role in AI chips and national security made the CEO question one of strategic importance. Some Nvidia employees said privately they were troubled by the precedent.

RSF drones strike Khartoum for a fourth day, killing four and damaging a UN building. An RSF commander defects.

Rapid Support Forces drones struck residential neighborhoods, Khartoum International Airport, and the headquarters of the Food and Agriculture Organization in a fourth consecutive day of attacks on Sudan's capital. Four civilians were killed, including a child, and roughly ten people were injured across Khartoum and the adjacent city of Omdurman. It was the first reported attack on UN premises in Khartoum since the Sudanese army recaptured the capital from the RSF in 2025. Sudan's military blamed the strikes on drones launched from Bahir Dar airport in Ethiopia. Ethiopia denied the accusation.

UN Secretary-General spokesperson Stephane Dujarric condemned the attacks and called for the protection of civilian infrastructure. Ambassadors from several countries and the EU delegation issued a joint statement calling any attack on civilian neighborhoods and humanitarian facilities unacceptable. In a separate development, RSF commander Ammar Mohammed Zein Rabih announced his defection to the Sudanese army at a press conference held at Khartoum airport, in what analysts said was a deliberate signal by the military that senior RSF officers who switch sides would receive a public welcome. Sudan's electrical grid remained at risk of collapse following earlier strikes on power stations.

NATO warns Russia may launch a nuclear-armed satellite by December. Rutte says collective defense extends to space.

Western intelligence agencies circulated warnings to NATO allies in early November that Russia could launch a satellite named Sputnik-S, designed to carry a nuclear warhead capable of disabling large numbers of satellites in low Earth orbit, as early as December. The assessment matched a separate evaluation by Germany's Armed Forces Space Command. A joint investigation by German broadcasters WDR and NDR and the Sueddeutsche Zeitung broke the story on Sunday. If launched and detonated, the weapon could endanger a third of the world's low-Earth-orbit satellites and would violate the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, which prohibits nuclear weapons in orbit.

NATO Secretary-General Rutte said Monday that the alliance's collective defense commitment extends to space and that any weapons deployment threatening allied satellites would be treated with the utmost seriousness. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz called the reports shocking but declined to confirm intelligence details, while Defense Minister Boris Pistorius urged the public to avoid speculation. Russia denied the characterization of Sputnik-S, calling it a research vehicle and saying its space activities comply with international obligations. No NATO member has publicly stated what it would do if Russia launched the satellite.
